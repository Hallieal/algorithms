Chapter 9 · Section 1

Greedy Strategy

Greedy algorithms commit to one locally best feasible choice at a time. The hard part is proving that the choice is safe.

Greedy means committing early

A greedy algorithm makes one choice, commits to it, and never comes back. That can be extremely efficient—but only when the problem has enough structure that the local choice cannot destroy every optimal solution.

RulePick the best feasible option now

The priority rule says what “best” means.

CommitmentDo not undo accepted choices

That irreversibility is what makes greedy fast—and risky.

Main questionWhy is this choice safe?

Without that proof, the rule is only a heuristic.

1

Look at the current state

Use only the information relevant to the decision you need to make now.

2

Choose locally

Pick the candidate with the best priority according to the greedy rule.

3

Keep it if feasible

The choice must not violate the constraints of the problem.

4

Commit and continue

Do not backtrack. Reduce the problem and repeat on what remains.

Definition

A greedy algorithm constructs a solution incrementally by repeatedly making a locally optimal feasible choice and never revisiting choices that have already been fixed.

Three things to identify

Before writing code, separate three ideas: which candidate comes first, whether it is allowed, and what becomes permanent after you accept it.

↕

Priority rule

Which candidate is considered first? Examples: smallest finishing time, cheapest edge, largest value density.

✓

Feasibility test

Can we take this candidate without breaking the constraints? If not, skip it or choose another candidate.

→

Irreversible commitment

Once accepted, a choice is treated as final. The algorithm does not enumerate alternative histories.

Important. “Choose the largest”, “choose the cheapest”, or “choose the nearest” is only a heuristic until we prove that the choice is safe.

Example: fit as many jobs as possible

Suppose a machine is available for 10 minutes. Four independent jobs take 7, 4, 3, and 2 minutes. The goal is to complete as many jobs as possible.

Greedy rule

Do the shortest remaining job first

Choosing a shorter job uses no more time than choosing a longer one, so it leaves at least as much room for future jobs.

The first three jobs use 2 + 3 + 4 = 9 minutes, so we complete 3 jobs. The remaining 7-minute job no longer fits.

Why this local choice is promising

If a solution contains a longer job while a shorter unchosen job exists, replacing the longer job by the shorter one cannot increase the total time. This exchange idea is exactly the kind of reasoning we need to justify a greedy rule.

A complete proof in three lines

Let d₁ ≤ d₂ ≤ ··· ≤ dₙ, and suppose greedy fits exactly the first k jobs. Then d₁+···+dₖ ≤ T but d₁+···+dₖ₊₁ > T. Any set of k+1 jobs has total duration at least that of the k+1 shortest jobs, so no solution can complete k+1 jobs.

A natural rule can still be wrong

A rule can be simple, deterministic, and always feasible—and still miss the optimum. Coin change gives a clean counterexample.

Coin change: take the largest coin that fits

For amount 6 with coins 1, 3, and 4, the greedy rule chooses 4 + 1 + 1: three coins. But 3 + 3 uses only two coins. The local rule is clear and feasible, yet it is not globally optimal.

Another failure: maximum matching

On the path 1—2—3—4, a rule that happens to choose the middle edge (2,3) first gets a matching of size 1.

The optimum

Choosing (1,2) and (3,4) gives size 2. The failure is not specific to coins: irreversible local choices can destroy future compatibility.

What a proof has to establish

A correct greedy proof normally has to justify two facts: the next local choice is safe, and after fixing it the remainder is still the same kind of problem.

Greedy-choice property

There exists an optimal solution that is compatible with the greedy first choice. In other words, committing to that local choice does not destroy every optimal solution.

Optimal substructure

After fixing the first choice, the rest of an optimal solution is itself optimal for the remaining subproblem.

Optimal substructure alone is not enough. Dynamic programming also relies on optimal substructure. What distinguishes a greedy algorithm is that one locally chosen branch can be committed to without keeping alternatives alive.

The code is usually the easy part

Once the priority rule is known, the implementation is often just sort, scan, test, accept.

2 ordered = sorted(candidates, key=priority)
3 solution = []
4 for candidate in ordered:
5 if feasible(solution, candidate):
6 solution.append(candidate)
7 return solution

What the skeleton means

2

Priority. Put candidates in the order dictated by the greedy rule.

4–6

Scan and commit. Consider each candidate once. If adding it remains feasible, accept it permanently.

This pattern is not universal: some greedy algorithms use a heap or repeatedly update priorities. But the conceptual structure is the same.

Typical running time

“Greedy” describes a design strategy, not a running-time class. Still, many greedy algorithms are fast because they avoid exploring many alternative solutions.

Implementation patternTypical costWhere it appears
Sort once + scanUsually O(n log n)Scheduling, interval selection, Kruskal-style processing
Priority queueOften O(n log n), or O((n + m) log n) on graphsHuffman coding, Prim, Dijkstra

Dynamic priorities / repeated updatesO(n²) with repeated scans; often O((n+u) log n) with a heap for u updatesGreedy rules whose priorities change after each choice

The exact bound always depends on the problem and data structures. A greedy algorithm can even be slow if its local choice is expensive to compute.

Exercises

1

Identify the greedy components

In the 10-minute job example, what are the priority rule, feasibility condition, and commitment?

Priority: shortest remaining job first. Feasibility: the job must fit in the remaining time. Commitment: once a job is selected, it stays in the schedule.
2

Break the coin heuristic

For coins 1, 5, and 6 and amount 10, what does “take the largest coin” return? What is optimal? Then define what it would mean for a coin system to be canonical.

Greedy gives 6 + 1 + 1 + 1 + 1, five coins. The optimum is 5 + 5, two coins. A coin system is called canonical when the largest-coin-first greedy algorithm gives a minimum-cardinality change for every target amount.
3

Feasible is not optimal

Why does checking feasibility after each choice not prove that the final solution is optimal?

Feasibility only says the partial solution obeys the constraints. It does not say that the choice preserves the possibility of reaching a globally best solution.
4

Complexity

If a greedy algorithm sorts n candidates and then checks each candidate in O(1), what is its total running time?

O(n log n): sorting dominates the subsequent O(n) scan.
