Greedy Strategy

Greedy algorithms build solutions through irreversible local choices. This page defines the method, distinguishes its guarantees, and gives a practical procedure for deciding when a greedy rule is justified.

Definition of a greedy algorithm

A greedy algorithm builds a solution one decision at a time. After a decision is accepted, it is not reconsidered. Correctness therefore depends on proving that each committed choice is compatible with some optimal solution.

RulePick the best feasible option now

The priority rule says what “best” means.

CommitmentDo not undo accepted choices

That irreversibility is what makes greedy fast—and risky.

Main questionWhy is this choice safe?

Without that proof, the rule is only a heuristic.

1

Current state

Use only the information relevant to the decision you need to make now.

2

Choose locally

Pick the candidate with the best priority according to the greedy rule.

3

Keep it if feasible

The choice must not violate the constraints of the problem.

4

Commit and continue

Do not backtrack. Reduce the problem and repeat on what remains.

Definition

A greedy algorithm constructs a solution incrementally by repeatedly making a locally optimal feasible choice and never revisiting choices that have already been fixed.

Exact algorithms, approximations, and heuristics

A procedure with greedy-looking local choices can have three different kinds of guarantees: exact optimality, a proved approximation bound, or no worst-case guarantee. These cases should be stated separately.

Exact algorithmAlways returns an optimum

Interval Scheduling, Fractional Knapsack, Huffman, MST, and Dijkstra belong here once their assumptions are satisfied.

HeuristicFast rule with no worst-case guarantee

A heuristic may be excellent in practice, but correctness is empirical rather than proved. Greedy ideas are often useful this way on hard problems.

ApproximationMay miss OPT, but by a proved amount

Greedy Set Cover is not exact, yet it comes with a mathematical approximation guarantee. That is much stronger than “usually good”.

Design principle. First decide which claim you are making: exact optimum, guaranteed approximation, or practical heuristic. The proof obligation is different in each case.

Reducing the search space

Many greedy algorithms become fast because they prove that most alternatives never need to be explored. The central question is which information can be discarded without changing the optimum.

Current idea

Compare every candidate against everything

For array maximum, checking each number against all others costs O(n²).

Better, but indirect

Sort, then take the last element

A comparison sort reduces this to O(n log n), but sorting computes far more order information than the problem asks for.

Best imaginable

Keep only the best seen so far

One scan is enough: update the current maximum whenever a larger value appears. That is O(n), and every input item must be inspected anyway.

Optimization question

What information is the slower algorithm computing that the final answer never uses? Greedy often becomes possible when that extra work can be replaced by one safe running summary.

Terminology

Scanning for a maximum is not usually classified as a greedy optimization algorithm. It is included here only as an example of state compression: after each prefix, one retained value summarizes all earlier candidates.

The three parts of a greedy rule

A greedy step consists of a priority rule, a feasibility test, and a commitment. Keeping these three roles separate makes both the implementation and the proof easier to state.

↕

Priority rule

Which candidate is considered first? Examples: smallest finishing time, cheapest edge, largest value density.

✓

Feasibility test

Can we take this candidate without breaking the constraints? If not, skip it or choose another candidate.

→

Irreversible commitment

Once accepted, a choice is treated as final. The algorithm does not enumerate alternative histories.

Important. “Choose the largest”, “choose the cheapest”, or “choose the nearest” is only a heuristic until we prove that the choice is safe.

Example: scheduling jobs within a time budget

A machine is available for 10 minutes. Four independent jobs take 7, 4, 3, and 2 minutes. The objective is to complete as many jobs as possible.

Greedy rule

Do the shortest remaining job first

Choosing a shorter job uses no more time than choosing a longer one, so it leaves at least as much room for future jobs.

Effect of the local choice

If a solution contains a longer job while a shorter unchosen job exists, replacing the longer job by the shorter one cannot increase the total time. This exchange idea is exactly the kind of reasoning we need to justify a greedy rule.

A complete proof in three lines

Let d₁ ≤ d₂ ≤ ··· ≤ dₙ, and suppose greedy fits exactly the first k jobs. Then d₁+···+dₖ ≤ T but d₁+···+dₖ₊₁ > T. Any set of k+1 jobs has total duration at least that of the k+1 shortest jobs, so no solution can complete k+1 jobs.

Similar local rules can have different guarantees

Interval scheduling, 0/1 Knapsack, and Set Cover all admit natural local choices, but the mathematical guarantees are different. A greedy-looking loop is not enough to determine whether the result is exact.

Exact

Classroom scheduling

Pick the compatible class that finishes first. The choice leaves at least as much room for every future class, so it can be proved globally optimal.

Greedy guess

0/1 Knapsack

Take the most attractive whole item first. This can look sensible and still lose to a combination of smaller items. Here greedy is only a heuristic unless an additional guarantee is proved.

Approximation

Set Cover

Pick the set covering the most still-uncovered elements. It may miss the exact optimum, but the loss can be bounded mathematically.

When judging an approximation

Ask two separate questions: how fast does it run, and how close is its answer guaranteed to be to OPT?

When judging a heuristic

Empirical quality is useful, but “it worked on all my examples” is not a worst-case guarantee.

The habit to keep

After proposing a greedy rule, immediately ask what kind of claim you are making.

Exact optimum? Provable approximation? Or simply a practical heuristic? The same-looking loop can belong to any of the three.

Counterexample: greedy coin change

For some coin systems, repeatedly taking the largest coin that fits does not minimize the number of coins. The example below shows why feasibility alone is not a correctness argument.

For amount 6 with coins 1, 3, and 4, the greedy rule chooses 4 + 1 + 1: three coins. But 3 + 3 uses only two coins. The local rule is clear and feasible, yet it is not globally optimal.

Another failure: maximum matching

On the path 1—2—3—4, a rule that happens to choose the middle edge (2,3) first gets a matching of size 1.

The optimum

Choosing (1,2) and (3,4) gives size 2. The failure is not specific to coins: irreversible local choices can destroy future compatibility.

Specify the problem before choosing a rule

The admissible inputs and the optimization objective must be fixed before a greedy rule can be evaluated. Small changes in the model can invalidate an otherwise correct argument.

Input class

What instances are allowed?

Are jobs single intervals or can one job contain gaps? Is the graph weighted or unweighted? Are edge weights nonnegative? The same-looking story can change algorithmically when the input class changes.

Objective

What does “best” mean?

Shortest route, fastest route, fewest turns, maximum number of jobs, minimum lateness, and minimum total network cost are different optimization problems.

Useful restriction

Can structure be narrowed?

Sometimes the right move is to exploit a restricted instance—intervals instead of arbitrary conflicts, trees instead of general graphs, nonnegative weights instead of arbitrary weights.

A good first sentence

“Before choosing a greedy key, I want to state exactly what inputs are allowed and what quantity is being optimized.” This prevents proving the right algorithm for the wrong problem.

A workflow for designing greedy algorithms

A practical design process is to propose a local rule, test it on small adversarial instances, identify the invariant that survives those tests, and then prove the corresponding safety statement.

1

Model the objective

State exactly what is feasible and what quantity is minimized or maximized. Tiny modeling changes can invalidate the whole rule.

2

Guess a local rule

Earliest finish? Largest density? Cheapest edge? Smallest tentative distance? Make the choice explicit enough to test.

3

Try to break it

Construct tiny adversarial examples. A single counterexample kills an exact greedy claim and often reveals what the correct rule must protect.

4

Name the invariant

What remains safe after the choice: more free time, an acyclic forest, a finalized distance, a feasible prefix, or a dominance frontier?

5

Prove or downgrade

Use exchange, staying ahead, induction, or a cut argument. If no proof exists, the rule may still be a heuristic or approximation—but call it that.

Counterexamples are part of the design process

Trying earliest start and shortest duration before discovering earliest finish is not wasted effort. The failed rules explain what information matters and make the final proof easier to see.

Reduce the instance after each choice

After accepting a greedy choice, remove the options that are no longer feasible or relevant. The remaining input should form a smaller instance to which the same rule applies.

1

Choose

Among the currently feasible options, identify the candidate favored by the greedy key: earliest finish, largest density, lightest safe edge, smallest frequency, and so on.

2

Commit

The decision becomes part of the partial solution. A correct greedy algorithm does not later backtrack and replace it.

3

Reduce

Delete incompatible or dominated possibilities and view what remains as a smaller instance of the same problem.

4

Repeat

The proof must justify that the same local rule remains safe on every reduced instance, not only on the first step.

What greediness buys

Once a decision is proved safe, the algorithm does not spend time reconsidering it. This often leads to short implementations and very small state.

What greediness risks

If the commitment is not actually safe, there is no recovery mechanism. A locally attractive choice can permanently destroy the global optimum.

Greedy is not “ignore the future”. The implementation may not explore future branches, but the correctness proof must explain why the future cannot punish the committed choice.

What correctness requires

An exact greedy proof must justify the current choice and the reduced problem. The choice must be safe, and the remaining subproblem must still have the structure assumed by the algorithm.

Greedy-choice property

There exists an optimal solution that is compatible with the greedy first choice. In other words, committing to that local choice does not destroy every optimal solution.

Optimal substructure

After fixing the first choice, the rest of an optimal solution is itself optimal for the remaining subproblem.

Optimal substructure alone is not enough. Dynamic programming also relies on optimal substructure. What distinguishes a greedy algorithm is that one locally chosen branch can be committed to without keeping alternatives alive.

Implementation pattern

Once the order and feasibility condition are known, the code is often a sort followed by one scan, or a priority queue that exposes the next candidate.

1def greedy(candidates, priority, feasible):
2 ordered = sorted(candidates, key=priority)
3 solution = []
4 for candidate in ordered:
5 if feasible(solution, candidate):
6 solution.append(candidate)
7 return solution

What the skeleton means

2

Priority. Put candidates in the order dictated by the greedy rule.

4–6

Scan and commit. Consider each candidate once. If adding it remains feasible, accept it permanently.

This pattern is not universal: some greedy algorithms use a heap or repeatedly update priorities. But the conceptual structure is the same.

Running time

Greedy is a design method, not a complexity class. Its running time comes from the operations used to order candidates, test feasibility, and maintain the current state.

Implementation patternTypical costWhere it appears
Sort once + scanUsually O(n log n)Scheduling, interval selection, Kruskal-style processing

The exact bound always depends on the problem and data structures. A greedy algorithm can even be slow if its local choice is expensive to compute.

Greedy summaries in one-pass algorithms

Some greedy algorithms do not select objects from a sorted list. Instead, they maintain a small summary of the processed prefix and discard states that can no longer improve any future answer.

best_so_far

Array maximum keeps only the greatest value seen. Every smaller previous candidate is permanently dominated.

current_sum

For maximum subarray, once the running sum becomes negative, carrying it forward can only hurt every future extension. Resetting it discards a dominated prefix.

low, middle

For an increasing triplet, continually lowering the first and second thresholds makes it easier—not harder—to find a later third value.

State compression

The interesting question is not “does this problem involve sorting?” but “can many partial histories be replaced by one stronger summary without losing a future optimum or witness?”

How to approach a new greedy problem

Start from the objective and the constraints. Then identify the candidate order, the information that must be preserved, and the statement that would make one choice irreversible.

1

State the objective

What exactly must be maximized, minimized, reached, or allocated? Similar-looking inputs can require different greedy keys.

2

Name the local choice

Say what gets committed now: move a destination frontier, discard candidate starts, satisfy one directional constraint, or pick the next priority item.

3

Explain what is discarded

The strongest greedy explanations say which alternatives no longer matter and why they cannot produce a better future.

4

Finish with complexity

Identify the number of passes and the auxiliary state. Many interview greedy solutions are valuable precisely because they collapse to O(n) time and O(1) extra space.

What a strong verbal explanation sounds like

“I will maintain this frontier/candidate/resource summary. When condition X happens, every discarded alternative is dominated because of Y. Therefore I can commit without backtracking.”

Common structural patterns

Greedy solutions often arise when one partial state dominates the alternatives, when a cut or exchange certifies a safe choice, or when a priority queue maintains the next admissible candidate.

Reaching a destination

Move the goal backward

In Jump to the End, keep the leftmost index already known to reach the current destination. If i + nums[i] ≥ destination, index i becomes the new destination. The suffix is compressed into one frontier.

Circular feasibility

Eliminate a block of starts

In Gas Stations, once a start fails at station b, every station between that start and b can be rejected together. The algorithm jumps directly to b+1.

Neighbor constraints

Solve one direction at a time

In Candies, the left-neighbor and right-neighbor requirements are handled in opposite passes. The second pass keeps the larger amount already required by either direction.

The shared signal

Look for a statement of the form “all these candidates are no better than this one summary.” A backward frontier, a surviving start index, or a directional lower bound can replace many explicit possibilities.

Exercises

1

Identify the greedy components

In the 10-minute job example, what are the priority rule, feasibility condition, and commitment?

Priority: shortest remaining job first. Feasibility: the job must fit in the remaining time. Commitment: once a job is selected, it stays in the schedule.
2

Break the coin heuristic

For coins 1, 5, and 6 and amount 10, what does “take the largest coin” return? What is optimal? Then define what it would mean for a coin system to be canonical.

Greedy gives 6 + 1 + 1 + 1 + 1, five coins. The optimum is 5 + 5, two coins. A coin system is called canonical when the largest-coin-first greedy algorithm gives a minimum-cardinality change for every target amount.
3

Feasible is not optimal

Why does checking feasibility after each choice not prove that the final solution is optimal?

Feasibility only says the partial solution obeys the constraints. It does not say that the choice preserves the possibility of reaching a globally best solution.
4

Classify the claim

A fast rule gives solutions within 10% of optimum on every test file you tried, but you have no proof. Is it an exact algorithm, approximation algorithm, or heuristic?

As stated, it is a heuristic. Experimental quality is useful evidence, but an approximation algorithm requires a proved worst-case bound, and an exact algorithm requires a proof of optimality.
5

Complexity

If a greedy algorithm sorts n candidates and then checks each candidate in O(1), what is its total running time?

O(n log n): sorting dominates the subsequent O(n) scan.
