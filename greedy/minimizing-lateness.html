Minimizing Maximum Lateness

On one machine, minimizing the worst lateness has a crisp greedy rule: do the earliest deadline first.

Put the most urgent deadline first

Every job has a processing time and a deadline. We must run all jobs on one machine. The objective is not to finish everything as early as possible; it is to make the worst deadline miss as small as possible.

RuleEarliest deadline first

Sort by deadline and never idle while work is available.

Why it worksSwap every deadline inversion

Putting the earlier deadline first cannot increase the worst lateness.

CostO(n log n)

The sort dominates the final linear scan.

Objective

If job j completes at time Cⱼ, its lateness is Lⱼ = Cⱼ − dⱼ. We want an ordering of all jobs that minimizes Lmax = maxⱼ Lⱼ.

Lateness may be negative. A job that finishes before its deadline has negative lateness. The objective cares about the worst value over all jobs.

Same schedule, different question, different greedy key

A useful greedy habit is to separate the picture of the input from the objective. Intervals and jobs can look almost identical on a timeline while asking for a completely different optimization.

Interval Scheduling

Some jobs may be rejected. We maximize how many survive, so finishing early protects future compatibility.

→

Maximum lateness

Every job must run. We minimize the worst deadline violation, so the critical information is the deadline rather than the finish time of the interval itself.

Try the rule before seeing it

What should move to the front: the shortest job, the earliest-starting job, or the earliest deadline?

Only one of these priorities is tied directly to the quantity we are minimizing. The proof section will show why that link is enough.

Transfer the question, not the old answer. Greedy algorithms are easy to misuse when two problems share the same story but optimize different quantities.

Scheduling notation makes the assumptions visible

Scheduling rules are unusually sensitive to tiny changes in the model. A useful habit is to name the machine environment, extra constraints, and objective before choosing an ordering.

1 || Lmax

1 means one machine; the empty middle field means all jobs are available at time 0 with no extra processing constraints; Lmax means minimize the maximum lateness. For exactly this model, ordering by nondecreasing deadline is optimal.

Change the objective

Shortest-processing-time first is excellent for some average waiting-time objectives, but it is not the theorem here. With jobs (p,d)=(1,100) and (2,2), SPT runs the 1-unit job first and gets L_max=1; EDF runs the deadline-2 job first and gets L_max=0.

Change job availability

Adding release times changes the notation to 1|r_j|L_max and changes the computational problem itself. A rule proved for 1||L_max cannot simply be carried over.

Model first, rule second. In scheduling, the same jobs can require entirely different algorithms when you change preemption, release times, machine count, or objective.

Algorithm: Earliest Deadline First

Sort jobs by nondecreasing deadline and run them back-to-back. This rule is usually called Earliest Deadline First (EDF) or Earliest Due Date (EDD).

1

Sort by deadline

Arrange jobs so that d₁ ≤ d₂ ≤ ··· ≤ dₙ.

2

Process without idle time

Start the next job immediately when the current one finishes.

3

Track completion times

After each job, cumulative processing time is its completion time.

4

Measure the worst lateness

Compute Cⱼ-dⱼ and take the maximum.

Why no idle time? When all jobs are already available, inserting idle time only delays one or more completion times. It cannot reduce maximum lateness.

Why sorting by deadline is safe

If two consecutive jobs appear in the wrong deadline order—i before j but dᵢ > dⱼ—swap them. The pair finishes at the same time as before, while the earlier-deadline job finishes sooner.

Before
i · later deadline
j · earlier deadline
→
After
j · earlier deadline
i · later deadline

Let both jobs start after some already-fixed prefix ending at time S. Before the swap, job j finishes at S+pᵢ+pⱼ. After the swap, job i finishes at exactly that same time, while j finishes earlier.

After swapping: job j

L'ⱼ = S+pⱼ-dⱼ ≤ S+pᵢ+pⱼ-dⱼ = Lⱼ.

So the earlier-deadline job cannot become later than it was before.

After swapping: job i

L'ᵢ = S+pⱼ+pᵢ-dᵢ ≤ S+pᵢ+pⱼ-dⱼ = Lⱼ, because dᵢ > dⱼ.

Thus neither swapped job creates lateness larger than the old lateness of j.

Finish the proof

Repeatedly swap adjacent deadline inversions. Maximum lateness never increases, and eventually the schedule is sorted by deadline. Therefore an EDF schedule is optimal.

Turn repeated choice into one preprocessing step

The rule “run the remaining job with the earliest deadline” could be implemented by searching from scratch after every completion. That is correct—but unnecessarily slow.

Repeated search

Find the minimum deadline again and again

If we scan all remaining jobs for the next deadline at every step, selection alone can take O(n²).

Sort once

Put deadlines in the required order up front

Sorting costs O(n log n). After that, completion times and lateness are computed in one linear scan.

Already sorted

The scheduling phase is O(n)

If jobs arrive in nondecreasing deadline order, the optimization itself needs only cumulative processing time and the current maximum lateness.

State you actually need during the scan

Just two running values: current time t and the largest lateness seen so far. Once the order is fixed, the rest of the schedule history is irrelevant.

Python implementation

1def minimize_max_lateness(jobs):
2 jobs = sorted(jobs, key=lambda job: job[1])
3 time = 0
4 max_lateness = float("-inf")
5 for processing, deadline in jobs:
6 time += processing
7 lateness = time - deadline
8 max_lateness = max(max_lateness, lateness)
9 return max_lateness

Interview recognition: verify the model before reusing EDF

Greedy interview mistakes often come from recognizing a familiar story too early. Before saying “sort by deadline”, confirm that the exact assumptions behind the exchange argument are still present.

Good match

One machine, all jobs ready now

Every job must be processed exactly once, there are no release times, and the objective is maximum lateness. This is the clean 1||L_{max} setting.

Objective changed

Average completion or waiting time

The same list of jobs may need a different ordering rule. Do not keep EDF just because deadlines still appear in the input.

Constraints changed

Release times, preemption, or multiple machines

These additions change what swaps are legal and what state matters. Re-derive the algorithm rather than transplanting the old greedy key.

Thirty-second explanation

“I want to eliminate deadline inversions. If two adjacent jobs are ordered with a later deadline before an earlier one, swapping them does not increase the maximum lateness. Repeating that removes all inversions, so nondecreasing deadlines are optimal.”

Change the model, change the algorithm

EDF is optimal for this exact objective and assumptions. Add release times or change what is being minimized, and the proof no longer applies automatically.

Release times make the problem fundamentally harder

With release dates, 1|r_j|L_max is strongly NP-hard in general. This is not a matter of finding a slightly smarter deadline sort.

Different objective

Minimizing total completion time, total weighted tardiness, or the number of late jobs are different scheduling problems with different optimal rules or harder algorithms.

Naive EDF among available jobs

J₁=(r=0,p=10,d=50), J₂=(r=1,p=1,d=2), J₃=(r=0,p=1,d=100). At time 0, J₂ is unavailable, so a non-idling EDF rule starts J₁. J₂ then completes at 11 and has lateness 9.

Better schedule

Idle until time 1, run J₂ from 1 to 2, then J₁ and J₃. The maximum lateness is 0. With release times, deliberate idling can be necessary.

Exercises

1

Compute lateness

A job has deadline 8 and completes at time 11. What is its lateness?

Its lateness is 11 − 8 = 3.
2

Early completion

A job has deadline 10 and completes at time 7. Is its lateness 0?

No. Lateness is 7 − 10 = −3. Tardiness would be max(0, L) = 0, but lateness itself may be negative.
3

Swap argument

Why does swapping an inverted adjacent pair not affect completion times of jobs after the pair?

The two jobs have the same total processing time in either order, so the time at which the pair finishes is unchanged. Every later job therefore starts at the same time as before.
