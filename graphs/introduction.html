Introduction to Graphs

Graphs are built for relationships. They let us turn routes, friendships, debts, links, and other connections into a structure that an algorithm can work with.

Spell checkingFind a real word requiring the fewest edits.
Social networksFind the closest doctor in your network.
Web crawlingFollow links from page to page.

Start with a real problem

Suppose you are in San Francisco and want to travel from Twin Peaks to the Golden Gate Bridge by bus. Your goal is to make as few transfers as possible. There are several possible routes, so how do you systematically find the one with the fewest steps?

Begin with everything reachable in one step. The bridge is not there. Next consider everything reachable in two steps. Still no bridge. At three steps, the Golden Gate Bridge finally appears. Other routes may reach it in four steps, but the first time it appears tells us that a three-step route is shortest.

Shortest-path problem. “Shortest” does not have to mean physical distance. It can mean fewest transfers, fewest edits, fewest links, or any other quantity represented by steps in a graph.

This suggests a general two-part strategy: first model the problem as a graph; then run an algorithm that searches the graph. Later we will use breadth-first search for exactly this kind of shortest-step problem.

A graph can model who owes whom

For another example, imagine a poker game. We want to record who owes money to whom. If Alex owes Rama money, draw an arrow from Alex to Rama. If Rama owes Adit, draw another arrow from Rama to Adit. The full picture is a graph of debts.

This example exposes the two ingredients of every graph: nodes and edges. A node can connect directly to several other nodes. In a directed graph, the direction of an edge also lets us distinguish incoming and outgoing neighbors.

Vertices and edges

A graph consists of vertices (also called nodes) and edges. A vertex represents an object. An edge represents a direct relationship between two objects. Two vertices joined by an edge are adjacent, or neighbors.

Vertex: one object, such as a person, station, or web page.

Edge: one direct connection, such as a friendship or road.

Neighbors: vertices connected directly by an edge. Here u has neighbors v and w; x is not a neighbor of u.

Example: a social network

Now consider a social network in which friendship is mutual. One straightforward representation is simply a list of friendship pairs:

friendships = [
    ["Alice", "Bob"],
    ["Bob", "Cynthia"],
    ["Alice", "Diana"],
    ["Bob", "Diana"],
    ["Elise", "Fred"],
    ["Diana", "Fred"],
    ["Fred", "Alice"],
]

Each pair represents one friendship. The problem is that this format does not give us Alice's friends directly. Alice might occur anywhere in the list, so finding all of her friendships requires scanning the relationships. A graph-oriented representation makes the connections much easier to work with.

friends = {
    "Alice": ["Bob", "Diana", "Fred"],
    "Bob": ["Alice", "Cynthia", "Diana"],
    "Cynthia": ["Bob"],
    "Diana": ["Alice", "Bob", "Fred"],
    "Elise": ["Fred"],
    "Fred": ["Alice", "Diana", "Elise"],
}

print(friends["Alice"])
# ['Bob', 'Diana', 'Fred']

Now Alice's neighbors are available directly with friends["Alice"]. The dictionary maps each person to the people adjacent to that person. This is the basic idea behind an adjacency list, which we will study carefully in the representation section.

Graphs and trees

Graphs can look a lot like trees because both consist of nodes connected to other nodes. In fact, every tree is a graph, but not every graph is a tree. Two restrictions distinguish a tree here: it has no cycles, and all of its nodes belong to one connected structure.

In the social graph above, Alice, Diana, and Bob form a cycle: Alice is connected to Diana, Diana to Bob, and Bob back to Alice. That is perfectly legal in a graph, but it prevents the structure from being a tree.

Connected and disconnected graphs

A tree also cannot have a piece that is disconnected from the rest. A general graph can. Imagine two separate pairs of friends and, in addition, Vicky, who has just joined the network and has no friends yet. All five people are vertices of the same graph even though no path joins all of them.

A graph is connected when every vertex is connected, directly or indirectly, to every other vertex. Otherwise it is disconnected.

Directed graphs: when the relationship is one-way

Friendship is naturally symmetric, but many relationships are not. If Alice follows Bob, Bob does not automatically follow Alice. For such situations we use a directed graph: every edge has an arrow.

followees = {
    "Alice": ["Bob", "Cynthia"],
    "Bob": ["Cynthia"],
    "Cynthia": ["Bob"],
}

Incoming and outgoing neighbors

In a directed graph, direction matters. If there is an arrow u → v, then v is an out-neighbor of u, while u is an in-neighbor of v. Vertices can be connected by a longer path without being neighbors: “neighbor” means a direct edge.

The modeling habit

When a problem talks about relationships, routes, dependencies, links, transformations, or who can reach whom, try drawing a graph. Ask two questions first: what are the vertices? and what does an edge mean? Then decide whether the edges are directed and whether they need weights. The rest of this chapter develops algorithms for the graph you have modeled.
