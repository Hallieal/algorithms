Flow Networks and Residual Graphs

A flow network is a directed graph whose edges have capacities. The residual graph records how much more flow can be sent and how much of the current flow can be cancelled.

Flow networks

Start with a directed graph. Choose one vertex as the source s, one as the sink t, and assign a nonnegative capacity to every directed edge.

G=(V,E)the directed network
c(u,v)capacity of edge u→v
f(u,v)flow currently sent through it
|f|total amount sent from s to t

The capacity of an edge is the maximum amount that may pass through it. Thus c(u,v)=7 allows any flow from 0 to 7 on u→v.

This section uses directed graphs, paths, adjacency lists, and BFS/DFS.

Feasible flow

A flow is feasible when it satisfies two conditions.

Capacity constraint

An edge cannot carry a negative amount and cannot exceed its capacity.

0 ≤ f(u,v) ≤ c(u,v)
Flow conservation

At every vertex other than s and t, total inflow equals total outflow.

Σu f(u,v)=Σw f(v,w)
The conservation rule applies to every vertex except s and t. The source has net outflow, and the sink has net inflow.

Reading a flow

Once a flow is chosen, it is convenient to label each edge as f/c: current flow first, capacity second.

4 / 5
means: 4 units are used out of capacity 5

The value of a flow is the total amount leaving the source. By conservation, the same amount enters the sink.

|f|=Σvf(s,v)=Σuf(u,t)

The maximum-flow problem asks for a feasible flow with the largest possible value.

Residual networks

Suppose an edge has capacity 10 and currently carries 6 units. From this state, four more units may be sent forward, and up to six of the existing units may be cancelled.

Residual graph

Forward residual capacity

How much additional flow can still be sent on the original edge.

cf(u,v)=c(u,v)-f(u,v)
Reverse residual capacity

How much of the current flow can be cancelled.

cf(v,u)=f(u,v)

Residual graph. For every direction with positive residual capacity, include the corresponding residual edge. The resulting graph describes the changes that are currently possible.

Why reverse edges matter

The first path chosen by an algorithm need not belong to the final flow. Reverse residual edges allow later augmentations to reroute flow sent earlier.

A reverse residual edge is bookkeeping. Traversing it cancels flow that was previously sent along the corresponding original edge.

Augmenting paths

An augmenting path is a path from s to t in the residual graph such that every edge on the path has positive residual capacity.

The whole path can be increased only as much as its smallest residual capacity:

Δ=mine∈P cf(e)

Here Δ=4. Pushing four units along the path increases the total flow by four and changes the residual graph.

Three objects are involved: the original network gives the capacities, the current flow gives the present assignment, and the residual graph gives the admissible changes to that assignment.
